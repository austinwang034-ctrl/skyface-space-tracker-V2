Each SPHEREx exposure measures one wavelength at each position, so a spectrum builds up from many exposures. Click anywhere in the tracker to get the spectrum there.

Molecules absorb or give off light at their own wavelengths. Pick one below and the site adds together only the images taken at that wavelength, then compares them with images taken just beside it. Whatever stands out in the difference is where that molecule is at work.

Astrometry is the measuring of positions. Each image is checked against Gaia stars moved to the exposure date, and pointing errors are removed. With the known stars pinned down, every other source in the image gets coordinates too. Sources with no Gaia match are listed as candidates.

Is it a known asteroid or comet?

NASA JPL keeps the orbits of every catalogued asteroid and comet. This asks which of them were near the patch when the image now showing in the tracker was taken.

NEOSSat: a second telescope on the same patch

NEOSSat is the Canadian Space Agency’s 15 cm space telescope. It has photographed chosen targets in visible light since 2013, so where it has looked it gives a much longer time span than SPHEREx.

Oldest first. NEOSSat images are not rotated North-up and its pointing can be off by a few pixels, so stars may jump between images.

    Why do things in the sky move, and how can you help find new ones?

    Asteroids and comets orbit the Sun. Nearby stars drift across the sky as they orbit the Galaxy (their proper motion). Because Earth orbits the Sun, nearby objects also shift back and forth during the year (parallax), which tells us how far away they are. Pluto was discovered when an astronomer compared two photographs and noticed that one point of light had moved.

    Comparing images taken at different times is still how new neighbours are found. Volunteers have discovered asteroids, comets, stars and brown dwarfs through projects like backyardworlds.org. Track any patch of sky, open Compare epochs, and look for a blue-and-red pair: that is something that moved.

    Where the data comes from, and who can use this site

    Every image in the tracker is a NASA SPHEREx observation, fetched live from NASA’s public archive at the moment you ask. The site is free, needs no account, and stores nothing about you.
